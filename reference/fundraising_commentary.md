# Commentary on the Fundraising and Federal Tax Compliance Policy
### PVHS Band Boosters, Inc.

**Status: explanatory only. Not adopted by the Board and not part of any policy.** Nothing here creates or limits an obligation. Where this Commentary and the Policy differ, the Policy governs. Where the Policy and the underlying law differ, the law governs.

Section numbers below correspond to the Policy sections they explain.

---

## 1. Why the corporation does not credit fundraising to individual families — Policy §2

This is the question that returns every year, and it deserves an answer with authority behind it rather than the Board's say-so.

**The standard.** An organization is not operated exclusively for exempt purposes "unless it serves a public rather than a private interest." Treas. Reg. §1.501(c)(3)-1(d)(1)(ii). *Private benefit* is broader than *inurement*: it does not require that the beneficiary be an insider. Benefit flowing to a defined class of private individuals defeats exemption unless it is incidental both qualitatively and quantitatively.

**The case.** *Capital Gymnastics Booster Club, Inc. v. Commissioner*, T.C. Memo. 2013-193 (Aug. 26, 2013). The Tax Court sustained the revocation of a booster club's exemption — the club had been recognized as exempt since 1988 — on facts that closely track what is usually proposed in booster fundraising-credit discussions:

- Mandatory assessments of "$600 to $1,400 per year per child," payable in cash **or** worked off through fundraising;
- Points worth $10 each, awarded in proportion to the profit a family generated and credited against that family's assessment;
- About 110 of 240 member families — roughly 46% — participated. The club "awarded $32,920 of the net profit, or approximately 93%, to families that participated in the fundraising or who filled board positions." Non-participating families received nothing and paid in full.

The court's objection was precisely the feature that makes credit systems attractive to the parents who use them: the club "rigorously assured that its fundraising did not generally benefit all the child-athletes in its programs but rather benefited only the children of parents who did the fundraising."

**What the same opinion says is acceptable.** Two consecutive sentences read as a specification for a compliant booster club:

> "Nor is this a circumstance in which the individual's contribution of his share of the cost is optional or where scholarships are made available for those who cannot afford the cost. Nor is this a circumstance in which every member is required to perform fundraising and no one can buy his way out; rather, the fundraising was an option chosen by those who wanted to earn their assessments."

Two things follow. First, the corporation's hardship-waiver and scholarship program is not merely charitable good practice — the court treated availability of assistance as a distinguishing feature. Second, the permitted designs in Policy §2 are not a grudging minimum; they are what the court described as the other side of the line.

**IRS analysis specific to booster clubs.** PMTA 2011-04 (POSTS-117415-10, Nov. 24, 2010), "Booster Club Fundraising," addresses clubs that charge dues and let participants either pay or earn credits, and analyzes both the exemption question and the participants' deduction. It is short and worth reading before any board discussion of this topic.

**One risk that is *not* law.** A design that reduces a family's own obligation in exchange for that family's labor also raises a question of taxable income to that family under IRC §61. **No decided case so holds, and PMTA 2011-04 does not reach it.** This is the corporation's own assessment of risk, offered as a reason for caution. It should not be presented to members as settled law.

## 2. Why §4958 matters more than revocation — Policy §3

Revocation is an organizational consequence and a distant one. IRC §4958 is personal and immediate, which is why Policy §3 leads with it.

Officers and Directors do not get to argue that a small booster club's officers lack real influence: voting members of the governing body, the president, and the treasurer are in a position to exercise substantial influence *per se* under Treas. Reg. §53.4958-3(c).

The taxes run against individuals: 25% of the excess benefit on the person who received it, plus a correction obligation; 10% on each organization manager who knowingly participated in approving it, capped per transaction; 200% if the excess benefit is not timely corrected. Rates and the cap are in the Schedule of Figures.

None of this is reached by Bylaws Article XII indemnification or by the director-liability provisions of chapter 617, Florida Statutes. Those address liability *to the corporation and to third parties*; an excise tax owed to the United States is neither.

## 3. Deductibility — Policy §4

**Why the Policy avoids saying "not deductible."** The flat statement overstates the law and is easy to attack, and once attacked it discredits the rest of the document. The accurate rule is that a payment is deductible only to the extent it exceeds the fair market value of the return benefits and the payer intended the excess as a gift. *United States v. American Bar Endowment*, 477 U.S. 105 (1986); *Hernandez v. Commissioner*, 490 U.S. 680 (1989).

Applied here: fair share and trip assessments are set to cover the participant's own costs, and the return benefits — instruction, staff, transportation, uniforms, entry fees, meals — ordinarily equal or exceed the payment. So the practical answer is that no deduction is available, but it is reached by applying a rule rather than by asserting a conclusion. The IRS reached the same result for booster clubs in PMTA 2011-04: "considering the substantial return benefits provided by the organization, it is unlikely in this example that the participants would qualify for a charitable contribution deduction."

**Earmarked gifts.** A contribution over which the charity holds no discretion and control is treated as a gift to the designated individual and is not deductible. Rev. Rul. 62-113. This is why the corporation declines gifts earmarked for a named student — the refusal protects the donor from a deduction that would not survive examination, rather than merely simplifying the corporation's bookkeeping.

**The two acknowledgment thresholds are worded differently on purpose.** IRC §6115 requires the quid pro quo disclosure for payments *in excess of* its threshold; IRC §170(f)(8) requires contemporaneous written acknowledgment for contributions *of or above* its threshold. Neither is indexed for inflation, which makes them unusual among the figures in the Schedule.

**Donor-side rules changed for 2026.** For tax years beginning in 2026 the One Big Beautiful Bill Act introduced a deduction for non-itemizers (up to $1,000 single / $2,000 joint, cash gifts to qualifying charities), a 0.5%-of-AGI floor on itemizers' charitable deductions, and a 35% cap on the value of itemized deductions for taxpayers in the top bracket. The corporation may state that these changes exist. It should not calculate any member's result.

## 4. Unrelated business income — Policy §5

The common error is to treat IRC §513(i) sponsorship as the whole unrelated-business question because sponsorship is where the rules are most often written down. It is not the likeliest exposure. Concessions, spirit wear, and a permanent online store are the corporation's recurring commercial activity, and they rest on the volunteer labor exception of §513(a)(1) — which fails quietly the moment paid staff run the activity. That is why Policy §5 requires a record of who performed the work rather than a conclusion that volunteers did.

Note also that §513(a)(1) and §513(a)(3) are not 501(c)(3)-specific; they apply to any organization subject to IRC §511. Only the convenience exception of §513(a)(2) is limited by entity type.

## 5. Games of chance — Policy §6

**Florida is the binding constraint, and it is the one most often missed.** Section 849.0935, Florida Statutes, permits a qualifying exempt organization to conduct drawings by chance, but only "if the organization has complied with all applicable provisions of chapter 496 and this section," §849.0935(2). Solicitation-of-Contributions registration is therefore a condition of the raffle authorization itself, not a neighboring requirement.

It is unlawful for such an organization, §849.0935(4)(b) —

> "To require an entry fee, donation, substantial consideration, payment, proof of purchase, or contribution as a condition of entering the drawing or of being selected to win a prize. However, this paragraph does not prohibit an organization from suggesting a minimum donation or from including a statement of such suggested minimum donation on any printed material used in connection with the fundraising event or drawing."

The proviso matters as much as the prohibition. The statute does not forbid asking for money; it forbids *requiring* it. A conventional "tickets are $5 each" raffle is unlawful. A suggested $5 donation, printed as suggested, with a free entry available on identical terms to anyone who asks, is lawful. The difference is entirely in how the material is worded and how the table is run.

Disclosure requirements are at §849.0935(3), including §849.0935(3)(e) — "that no purchase or contribution is necessary" — which must be conspicuously disclosed on all brochures, advertisements, notices, tickets, and entry blanks. Violation is a deceptive and unfair trade practice, §849.0935(6), and a second-degree misdemeanor, §849.0935(7). The statute was last substantively amended in 2013.

**The federal trap is the noncash prize.** Regular gambling withholding is owed on proceeds above the withholding threshold, and it is the corporation's obligation whether or not it is collected from the winner. Where the prize is cash, the corporation withholds from it. Where the prize is a donated television, there is nothing to withhold from — the corporation pays the tax out of its own funds, on top of a prize it did not buy. Hence Policy §6 condition 6: name the budget line before approving the plan. And hence condition 7: the Form W-9 comes before the prize is released, because afterwards the corporation has no leverage.

**A caution about IRS publications.** The reporting threshold for Form W-2G changed for 2026 and is now indexed annually. IRS Publication 3079, "Tax-Exempt Organizations and Gaming," is the natural place to look this up, sits on irs.gov, and is revised 10-2018 — it still shows the former $600 figure. The first draft of this package repeated it. Use current-year *form instructions*; treat topic publications as background only.

## 6. Public disclosure — Policy §7

Two distinctions a treasurer is likely to miss.

**The window is per-return, not a running count.** The obligation attaches to each annual return during the three-year period beginning on that return's filing due date. It is not "the three most recent returns." The exemption application has no time limit at all.

**Posting online is only half the duty.** Making documents "widely available" on a website relieves the obligation to provide *copies*. It does not relieve the separate obligation to permit *inspection* at the principal office during business hours. A treasurer who posts the 990s and considers the matter closed has satisfied one of two duties.

Penalty amounts are deliberately not recited in the Policy or here. They are adjusted for inflation annually, and a document that names them reads as authoritative while being wrong within a year. The operative facts — per-day accrual, assessment against the individual responsible, and a separate flat penalty for willful failure — do not depend on the number.

## 7. Information returns — Policy §8

The Form 1099-NEC / 1099-MISC threshold rose from $600 to $2,000 for payments made after December 31, 2025, with inflation indexing beginning in 2027 (One Big Beautiful Bill Act §70433). This is live for the corporation because it pays Program staff and designers.

Three inferences a volunteer treasurer might draw from the change, all wrong: that a higher filing threshold loosens the worker-classification analysis (it does not); that a payment below the threshold is not income to the recipient (it is); and that keeping a worker below the threshold makes the arrangement invisible (it does not, and structuring payments toward that end is its own problem).

The same statutory change drives the Form W-2G threshold discussed in §5 above. If the two figures ever appear to diverge in the Schedule, one of them is wrong.
